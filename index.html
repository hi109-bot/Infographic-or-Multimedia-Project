Part I · Needs

Maslow’s Hierarchy
of Needs

Key theorist: Abraham H. Maslow

Motivation reflects needs ranging from basic survival to developing one’s potential.

  1. Five major needs: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.
    Example: A student seeks meals, safe housing, friendships, recognition, and opportunities to grow.
  2. Relative priority: unmet basic needs tend to command attention; their satisfaction can make room for higher needs.
    Example: After eating and resting, a student can give more attention to a creative project.
  3. Flexible progression: needs can overlap, and exceptions occur. A lower need does not require complete satisfaction before a higher need matters.
    Example: A student facing financial uncertainty can still seek friendship and personal achievement.

Source: Navy (2020).

Student example: A hungry, exhausted student may prioritize food and rest before focusing on an ambitious project.

Physiological Food, water, sleep, and other basic bodily needs.

Original five-level model: a flexible hierarchy, not a rigid staircase.

Later development: self-transcendence. Maslow later described motivation beyond personal fulfillment: serving others or a cause beyond oneself.
Example: A student uses their skills to organize a community food drive.

Part II · Value

Expectancy-Value
Theory

Key theorists: Jacquelynne S. Eccles & Allan Wigfield
Achievement motivation / situated expectancy-value approach

Choices depend on expectations of success and the subjective value of a task.

  1. Expectancy: “How likely am I to succeed?” Beliefs about future success influence engagement and performance.
    Example: A student who expects to succeed in statistics is more willing to enroll.
  2. Subjective task value: enjoyment, personal importance, usefulness, and perceived cost shape a task’s appeal.
    Example: A student enjoys accounting (intrinsic), sees competence as part of their identity (attainment), values career preparation (utility), and weighs study time (cost).
  3. Situated motivation: experiences, social messages, and cultural context help shape expectancies and values, influencing choices and persistence.
    Example: Encouragement from a mentor and positive class experiences can change which courses a student believes are possible and worthwhile.

Source: Eccles and Wigfield (2024).

Student example: A student may choose a difficult course because it supports a career goal, while weighing the time and stress it requires.
Intrinsic value Enjoyment or interest.
Attainment value Importance of doing well; relevance to identity.
Utility value Usefulness for goals.
Perceived cost Effort, stress, or lost alternatives.

Part III · Belief

Self-Efficacy

Within Social Cognitive Theory · Key theorist: Albert Bandura

Self-efficacy is belief in one’s capability to carry out the actions required for a particular task.

  1. Task-specific capability: believing you can perform an action differs from expecting that action to produce a desired outcome.
    Example: “I can solve these problems” is an efficacy belief; “solving them will improve my grade” is an outcome expectation.
  2. Effort and persistence: efficacy beliefs influence whether people attempt a challenge and how long they persist through obstacles.
    Example: A student who believes they can learn a technique may try another strategy after a failed attempt.
  3. Four information sources: successes, observing others, credible encouragement, and interpretations of bodily or emotional states shape efficacy beliefs.
    Example: A student completes a practice problem, watches a peer demonstrate one, receives specific encouragement, and learns to interpret nerves as manageable.
  4. Reciprocal interactions: personal factors, behavior, and the social environment influence one another.
    Example: Confidence encourages practice; practice leads to progress; teacher feedback strengthens confidence.

Source: Schunk and DiBenedetto (2020).

Student example: Successfully solving increasingly difficult practice problems can strengthen a student’s belief that they can handle similar exam tasks.

Part IV · Comparing theories

One challenge.
Three perspectives.

These theories offer complementary explanations, while emphasizing different motivational mechanisms.

Lens Maslow Expectancy-Value Self-Efficacy
Main focus Needs and their relative priority Expected success and task value Belief in task-specific capability
Central question Which needs are motivating me? Can I succeed, and is it worth it? Can I carry out the required actions?
Structure A flexible hierarchy of needs Expectancies and values shaped by context Efficacy beliefs shaped by four sources
Illustrative support Provide rest, safety, and belonging Connect tasks to goals and address costs Build success through guided practice
Internal experience matters. Needs, values, and capability beliefs each help explain behavior.
Context matters. Support and experiences can affect needs, task appraisals, or efficacy beliefs.
Motivation can change. Satisfaction, changing priorities, and learning experiences can shift what drives action.

Comparison and practical supports are a synthesis of Navy (2020), Eccles and Wigfield (2024), and Schunk and DiBenedetto (2020). Expectancy and self-efficacy overlap as ability beliefs. Expectancy-value theory foregrounds task value and cost; self-efficacy foregrounds perceived capability. The broader Social Cognitive Theory also includes values and outcome expectations.
